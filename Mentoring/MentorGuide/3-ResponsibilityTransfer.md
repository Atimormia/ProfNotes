# Mentor Guide 03: Responsibility Transfer Toolkit

## Purpose

This toolkit covers how a Mentor deliberately hands over ownership of a decision, a skill, or a tool relationship to a Mentee, in stages, rather than all at once or not at all. It applies to any area of responsibility: a new technology, an architectural decision area, a production ownership boundary, or a Mentee's use of AI assistance. AI is treated as one instance of this general model, not a separate process.

## Why This Needs to Be Deliberate

Handing over full responsibility instantly prevents the underlying judgment from ever forming, since the Mentee never had to build it under lower stakes first. Withholding responsibility indefinitely prevents the Mentee from ever finding out, and from anyone else finding out, whether they can actually hold it. Left unmanaged, most working relationships drift toward one of these two failure modes by default, since neither requires a deliberate decision, only the absence of one.

Some tools and technologies make this drift worse than others. A human collaborator naturally reduces their own support as they observe a Mentee's competence grow. A tool, including AI assistance, does not do this on its own. It remains equally available regardless of how much the Mentee actually needs it, which means the fade has to be imposed by the Mentor and Mentee on purpose, or it simply doesn't happen.

## Movement Is Not Always Forward

A Mentee can regress from Own It back to Do Alone or Do Together in a specific area, particularly after a genuine failure, a change in the underlying technology, or a long gap without practicing the skill. This is not a discipline failure on the Mentee's part or a diagnostic failure on the Mentor's. Treat a regression using the same Diagnostic Toolkit method used to advance a stage in the first place, rather than assuming stage assignment is permanent once reached.

## Deciding Readiness to Advance

Use the Diagnostic Toolkit's method (Guide 01) to gather the Exit Evidence for any stage; this toolkit does not introduce a separate instrument. Multiple sources matter more here than at a routine Checkpoint, since a Mentee's own report of their readiness is the least reliable single source available for this specific question. A Mentee eager to advance may overstate their own reasoning quality; a Mentee anxious about responsibility may understate it. Peer input and the Mentor's own review of the Mentee's actual proposed reasoning, not just the Mentee's account of it, are the more load-bearing sources at this toolkit's stage boundaries specifically.

## The AI Dimension

AI assistance is one column inside this model, evaluated at each stage exactly as any other tool or resource would be. It is not a parallel process requiring separate stages.

| Stage       | AI's Role                                                                                                                                           |
|-------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Watch       | The Mentor demonstrates where they use AI and where they override or ignore it. The Mentee sees it treated as one input, not an oracle.             |
| Do Together | The Mentee may use AI freely while proposing a decision; the Mentor reviews the reasoning behind the proposal, whether or not AI contributed to it. |
| Do Alone    | Every point where the Mentee deferred to AI must be named and justified in writing, exactly as any other deferral would be.                         |
| Own It      | The decision and its outcome are attached to the Mentee regardless of what tools were used to reach it.                                             |

The one caution specific to AI, worth naming because it does not apply equally to a human resource the Mentee might defer to: AI does not reduce its own availability as the Mentee's competence grows the way a Mentor naturally does. A Mentor should watch for a Mentee's AI usage staying flat or increasing across stages where it should be decreasing, since that pattern indicates the fade isn't happening on its own and needs to be enforced directly at the Do Alone stage's documentation requirement.

## Software Engineering Example

A Mentee is being brought up to ownership of a specific service's on-call response process. At Watch, the Mentor handles an incident live, narrating their diagnostic reasoning, including a moment where they asked an AI assistant to summarize an unfamiliar log format and then verified that summary against the raw logs themselves rather than trusting it outright. At Do Together, the Mentee proposes a response plan for a simulated incident; the Mentor reviews not whether the plan would work, but why the Mentee ruled out two other approaches first. At Do Alone, the Mentee handles a real, low-severity incident independently, documenting in writing that they used AI to draft an initial hypothesis but confirmed it manually before acting on it. At Own It, the Mentee is the named on-call owner for that service's incident class, and a subsequent incident's postmortem lists their name and reasoning directly, not "the on-call engineer."

## Result

A documented stage assignment per area of responsibility, the Exit Evidence that justified the Mentee's current stage, and a visible record of AI's changing role across those stages, feeding the Mentoring Strategy Document and the module-level records described in Guide 04.

## Addendum: Management-Track Mentoring, a Scope Note

Everything above describes responsibility transfer for individual-contributor mentoring: engineers, designers, artists, testers. It applies within that scope because IC mistakes are typically discoverable, reversible, and safely practiced on real, low-risk work.

Management-track mentoring, supporting someone newly promoted into people management, does not share that shape, and this guide does not currently extend the four-stage model to cover it in full.

**Structural Difference 1: Consequences.** A new manager's mishandled feedback conversation, poorly judged performance call, or mismanaged conflict affects another person's standing, often before anyone above the new manager is aware it happened. This is closer to the per-activity trust model used in high-stakes supervised professions than to the broader-area model used above in this guide. A management trainee may be ready to handle a routine one-on-one and not yet ready to handle a termination conversation; collapsing both into one general "management" stage assignment would hide a real, consequential gap.

**Structural Difference 2: Practice Safety.** A management trainee generally cannot practice a difficult conversation on a real, live report the way an engineering Mentee can be handed a real, low-risk task. The common mechanism used elsewhere for this instead is simulation: role-play of specific, well-known hard scenarios (delivering critical feedback, mediating a team conflict, having a hard prioritization conversation), case-based discussion, and structured self-assessment of leadership style, layered underneath ongoing mentorship from an experienced manager rather than substituted for it.

**Current Status:** A full management-track parallel to the toolkits in this guide is out of scope for this version. This should be built from direct experience mentoring people managers, the same way the toolkits above were built from direct experience mentoring engineers, rather than assembled secondhand.

**Starting Points for Future Development:** Role-play scenarios covering a narrow, known set of high-frequency hard conversations; case-based discussion rather than lecture; explicit support for the transition from being evaluated on personal output to being evaluated on team output; and per-activity trust judgments rather than one global readiness assessment. Established frameworks exist for structuring a manager's early transition period and for feedback-delivery specifically, and are worth reviewing before authoring original material in either area.
