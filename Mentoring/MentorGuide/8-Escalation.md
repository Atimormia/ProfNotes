# Mentor Guide 08: When Something Isn't Working

## Purpose

This toolkit covers what to do when friction in a mentoring relationship does not resolve on its own: a mismatch that isn't closing, a relationship that has stopped functioning, or a recurring conflict named in an earlier toolkit (Guide 07's pattern of mentoring losing to project priorities, for instance) that needs to go somewhere beyond the pairing itself. The original Program names "Conflict Resolution" as a responsibility handled with empathy and soft skills; this toolkit makes that concrete, since empathy alone does not tell a Mentor or Mentee when to act, or who to bring in.

## The Core Principle: Match the Response to the Evidence

The instinct when something feels wrong is to either escalate immediately or absorb the friction indefinitely rather than name it. Neither is correct by default. The right response is proportional to the evidence available, and evidence accumulates in stages. Escalating on a single bad session wastes organizational attention on noise; absorbing a recurring pattern indefinitely lets a real, fixable problem persist because naming it felt disproportionate the first few times it appeared.

## The Escalation Path

### Rung 1: Leave a Record

The lowest-cost response, and the correct default for a single instance of friction. Note what happened, briefly and factually, in the current Module Plan or Module Outcome Record (Guide 04), without requiring any conversation beyond the pairing itself. This costs almost nothing and carries no risk, but it means the instance is not lost if it turns out to matter later.

### Rung 2: Name the Transition Point Once

Where a single instance repeats, name it directly to the other party before it becomes a pattern neither side has acknowledged. This is a factual statement, not a confrontation: stating plainly what has been noticed and that it needs to change, said once, on the record in the relevant document. If the friction resolves after being named, nothing further is required. If nothing changes after being named clearly, that non-response becomes part of the evidence for the next rung, without the Mentor or Mentee needing to have pushed harder in the moment.

### Rung 3: Confirm Recurrence Before Escalating

This is the gate that separates a normal, isolated friction from something worth spending organizational attention on. A second occurrence, after Rung 2's transition point was already named, is what confirms a pattern rather than a one-off. Do not escalate past this point without recurrence confirmed against a Rung 2 record; escalating on a single instance, however uncomfortable, is not yet justified by the evidence.

### Rung 4: Widen the Scope

Once recurrence is confirmed, bring in the appropriate stakeholder, per the roles already defined in the original Program:

* **The Mentee's or Mentor's Manager**, where the friction concerns workload, project-priority conflict (Guide 07), or a mismatch in expectations that the pairing itself cannot resolve.
* **HR**, where the friction concerns the psychological or interpersonal dynamic of the relationship itself, including any of the patterns named in Guide 05 (affinity bias, a Mentee's or Mentor's defensive dynamic that has not improved) or Guide 06 (a Mentor's over-extension that workload reassignment alone hasn't addressed).
* **Both**, where the friction touches both the working relationship and organizational factors, such as a pattern of mentoring consistently losing to project priorities across multiple seasons (Guide 07).

Bring the Rung 1 and Rung 2 records with you. Widening the scope on a documented pattern is a straightforward request for support; widening it on an undocumented feeling is a much harder position for a stakeholder to act on.

### Rung 5: Treat the Response as Data

Whatever comes back, a resolution, a change in the pairing, a decision to end the mentoring relationship and rematch, or continued inaction, is information about the organization's actual priorities, not a verdict on the Mentor's or Mentee's conduct. A rematch is not a failure of the original pairing; it is sometimes the correct outcome of an honest diagnostic process. Continued inaction after a documented, recurring pattern has been raised is itself a data point worth recording in the HR Program Review (Guide 04), since a program experiencing this pattern across multiple pairings is showing something about its own structure, not about any one relationship.

## Matching Severity to the Right Entry Point

Not every situation needs to start at Rung 1. Use judgment about where to enter:

* A single missed session, a scheduling conflict, or an isolated disagreement: start at Rung 1.
* A repeated, minor friction the pairing can plausibly resolve itself: start at Rung 2.
* A safety concern, a bias pattern (Guide 05) affecting the Mentee directly, or a Mentor's over-extension (Guide 06) that is visibly affecting their wellbeing: skip directly to Rung 4. Evidence-gating exists to prevent premature escalation of ordinary friction, not to delay action on a genuine concern for someone's wellbeing.

## Result

A proportional response to mentoring friction, gated by accumulated evidence rather than by the intensity of frustration on either side, with a clear point at which the appropriate stakeholder (Manager, HR, or both) gets involved, and a way of reading whatever response comes back as organizational information rather than personal judgment.
