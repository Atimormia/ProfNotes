# Mentor Guide 06: Mentor Growth Toolkit

## Purpose

Mentoring is a two-way process. This toolkit covers what a Mentor should watch for in their own development, using the same diagnostic shape applied to Mentees elsewhere in this guide, since a Mentor's own patterns are just as recognizable, and just as easy to miss from the inside, as a Mentee's.

## Why This Belongs in the Framework, Not Outside It

A Mentor's growth is not a side benefit of running the program well. It is one of the program's stated goals (see the Mentoring Program's own goal of Leadership Development), and a Mentor who is not developing, or who is quietly depleted, is a real risk to the Mentee's development too, since most of the patterns below degrade the mentoring relationship silently, without the Mentor necessarily noticing.

---

## Pattern: Showcasing Instead of Teaching

**Early Signal:** A Mentor notices they enjoy demonstrating their own knowledge or solving a problem in front of the Mentee more than they enjoy watching the Mentee work through it themselves.

**Confirmed Pattern:** Sessions consistently center on what the Mentor knows or has done, rather than on the Mentee's own reasoning. The Mentee spends more time watching the Mentor perform competence than practicing it themselves.

**Approach:** Check session content against Guide 03's stage model directly. Watch is a legitimate stage, but only the first of four, and only for a Mentee genuinely early in that specific area of responsibility. If most sessions default to Watch regardless of the Mentee's actual stage, the Mentor is very likely optimizing for their own comfort rather than the Mentee's progression.

**Psychology Note:** This pattern is often unintentional and even well-meant. Demonstrating expertise is genuinely rewarding, and it is easy to mistake that reward for evidence of good teaching. The two are not the same thing, and only one of them serves the Mentee.

---

## Pattern: Answering Instead of Letting Struggle Happen

**Early Signal:** A Mentee asks a question, and the Mentor's reflex is to answer it directly and immediately, rather than to ask what the Mentee has already tried or considered.

**Confirmed Pattern:** The Mentor consistently resolves the Mentee's uncertainty for them rather than letting the Mentee sit with it, across sessions and across different kinds of questions, not just once under time pressure.

**Approach:** This is the single most consistently named distinction between a skilled and an unskilled mentor in the broader field: knowing when to answer and when to let the Mentee find the answer themselves. Before answering, ask the diagnostic question first, what has the Mentee already tried, and what is the actual gap type (Guide 01) behind this specific question. A knowledge gap may genuinely warrant a direct answer. A practice or judgment gap usually does not, and answering it directly quietly converts a Do Alone moment (Guide 03) back into a Do Together moment, undermining the fade the Mentor and Mentee already agreed to.

**Psychology Note:** Answering feels helpful in the moment and often is experienced as helpful by the Mentee too, which is exactly what makes this pattern hard to self-correct. The discomfort of watching someone struggle is the Mentor's own discomfort, not necessarily evidence that the Mentee needs rescuing.

---

## Pattern: Avoiding One's Own Gaps

**Early Signal:** A Mentor feels reluctant to say "I don't know" or "I'm not sure" in front of a Mentee, and finds themselves deflecting or changing the subject when a question touches an area of their own uncertainty.

**Confirmed Pattern:** The Mentor consistently avoids acknowledging uncertainty, guesses with unwarranted confidence rather than admitting a gap, or steers sessions away from topics where their own knowledge is thin.

**Approach:** Model the same behavior expected of the Mentee at Guide 03's Do Alone stage: name uncertainty explicitly rather than hiding it. A Mentor saying "I'm not certain, let's find out together" is not a credibility loss; it demonstrates exactly the honest handling of a gap the framework asks the Mentee to practice.

**Psychology Note:** A Mentor's own imposter pattern (see Guide 05) often shows up here specifically, since being asked a question one cannot answer, in front of someone being mentored, can trigger the same fear of being "found out" the Mentee may separately be experiencing. Recognizing this as one's own instance of a named pattern, rather than a unique personal failing, makes it easier to act against.

---

## Pattern: Over-Extension and the Need to Be Needed

**Early Signal:** A Mentor notices they are the one initiating extra sessions, taking on additional Mentees beyond what feels sustainable, or feeling a pull to solve problems the Mentee brings that fall outside the actual scope of the mentoring relationship.

**Confirmed Pattern:** A recurring pattern of fatigue, irritability, or declining patience specific to mentoring interactions, often alongside a Mentor privately feeling that turning down a request for their time or stepping back from over-involvement would mean letting the Mentee down.

**Approach:** Distinguish being a resource from being the resource. A Mentor's role is to guide the Mentee toward their own solutions and appropriate outside support, not to personally resolve everything the Mentee brings, including concerns beyond the mentoring relationship's actual scope. Where workload is the driver, use the program's own structural levers rather than absorbing the cost personally: the defined time allocation and workload limits set in the original Program's Phase 0, and the optional Mentor Training available in Phase 1, both exist specifically to prevent this pattern rather than leaving a Mentor to manage it through willpower alone.

**Psychology Note:** This pattern is not a discipline failure, and it disproportionately affects Mentors who are genuinely good at the role. The same empathy and compassion that make someone an effective mentor also make it harder for them to set the boundaries that would protect their own capacity to keep mentoring well. Being needed is itself rewarding, which is precisely what makes this pattern difficult to notice from the inside; it does not feel like overextension while it is happening; it feels like being helpful.

---

## Special Case: When the Mentor Shares the Mentee's Gap

Sometimes a gap diagnosed in a Mentee (Guide 01) turns out to be one the Mentor shares. This is a distinct scenario from ordinary uncertainty about a single question, and it deserves its own approach rather than being folded into general encouragement to admit gaps.

**Approach:** Name the shared gap to the Mentee directly, rather than quietly working around it or picking a different topic to avoid it. Where practical, treat this as an opportunity rather than a liability: the Mentor and Mentee can address the gap together, with the Mentor modeling exactly the kind of Reactive-Mode learning (Guide 02) or honest deferral practice (Guide 03) being asked of the Mentee elsewhere. Where the gap is significant enough that the Mentor cannot reasonably guide the Mentee through closing it, say so plainly and route the Mentee toward another resource, whether a curated third-party source (Guide 02, Step 0), a peer, or a different Mentor for that specific area, rather than quietly under-serving the Mentee to protect the pairing.

**Psychology Note:** A Mentor may feel this admission threatens their standing or the Mentee's confidence in them. In practice, the opposite is more commonly true: a Mentor who is transparent about a shared gap models the exact behavior the framework is trying to build in the Mentee, and a Mentee who sees this modeled is more likely to practice it themselves later.

---

## Two Standing Responsibilities

Beyond the specific patterns above, two responsibilities belong to the Mentor role continuously, not as a pattern to correct once but as an ongoing part of the job.

**Responsibility for Direction.** A Mentee working within a well-run module can still end up moving toward a goal that no longer serves them, if the Mentor is not periodically checking the accumulated direction of a season against the Mentee's actual goals (Guide 01's Individual Goals and Baseline Document) and against Guide 01's Season Synthesis. Good execution of individual modules does not guarantee good direction across a season; only the Mentor is positioned to notice if it drifts, since the Mentee is inside the work and the Manager typically only sees season-level results.

**Responsibility for Psychological Atmosphere.** The patterns in Guide 05 (imposter pattern, defensive response to feedback) are more likely to surface, and less likely to be worked through honestly, in a relationship where the Mentee does not feel safe being wrong in front of the Mentor. This is not a soft, secondary concern; it is a precondition for nearly everything else in this framework working as intended, since Guide 03's Do Alone stage specifically requires a Mentee willing to document their own deferrals and uncertainties honestly. A Mentor is responsible for actively building that safety, not assuming it exists by default because the Mentor has good intentions.

## Why Structure Protects the Mentor, Not Only the Mentee

Every pattern in this guide, and the toolkits it draws on, assumes a defined, staged, criteria-first approach to mentoring rather than open-ended, undefined availability. This is not only a discipline imposed for the Mentee's benefit. A defined structure, in the form of clear stage boundaries (Guide 03), agreed closure definitions (Guide 01), and bounded module scope (Guide 02), is also what allows a Mentor to know when a task is actually finished, rather than remaining perpetually, informally on call. A Mentor experiencing Over-Extension is often, in practice, operating without these boundaries clearly enforced, which is a further reason to apply the framework's existing structure rigorously rather than relaxing it under the impression that more availability is more helpful.

## Result

A set of five named patterns for self-diagnosis, matching the same signal-based method used elsewhere in this guide, and two standing responsibilities that apply across the full mentoring relationship rather than at any single stage or module. Sustaining the capacity to mentor well is treated here as a structural property of following the framework's existing boundaries, not as a separate personal discipline the Mentor must supply on top of it.
