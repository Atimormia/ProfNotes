# Mentor Guide 07: Balancing Teaching and Project Priorities

## Purpose

This toolkit covers a resource conflict rather than a diagnostic pattern: project delivery and mentoring both draw on the same finite time, from both the Mentor and the Mentee, and the two priorities compete by default rather than as an exception. Unlike Guides 05 and 06, this is not a signal-based pattern to watch for; it is a recurring decision that needs a consistent answer, since it will come up in nearly every module.

## Why This Cannot Be Resolved Once

A fixed rule set once at the start of a mentoring relationship ("mentoring always takes priority" or "project work always takes priority") does not survive real conditions. Project pressure is not constant, and neither is the value of a given module to the Mentee's development. This toolkit provides a repeatable way to make the call each time it arises, rather than a single standing rule.

## Deciding Which Tasks Belong to Mentoring

Not every piece of real project work is a good fit for mentoring purposes, and not every mentoring need is best served by real project work. The original Program's own criteria for identifying suitable project tasks remain the starting point: low priority or low risk to the project, paired with high learning potential relative to the Mentee's diagnosed gap (Guide 01). Where a task does not meet both halves of that criteria, it is not a good candidate for an In-Project Task (Guide 02, Step 3), regardless of how convenient its timing is.

A task can fail this test in either direction. A task that is genuinely low-risk to the project but does not address the Mentee's actual diagnosed gap wastes mentoring time on convenience rather than development. A task that addresses the gap well but carries real delivery risk or is on the critical path should not be reframed as a mentoring opportunity simply because it happens to be available; the Mentee bears the learning cost of struggling with it, but the project bears the cost of a wrong call, and those costs should not be quietly transferred onto the Mentee's plate under the label of development.

Where neither an In-Project Task nor a Nice-to-Have Project (Guide 02, Step 3) fits the diagnosed gap and the current project's state, a Dedicated Exercise is the correct default, not a compromise. A well-chosen Dedicated Exercise that cleanly isolates the gap is a better use of time than a real task that only loosely fits it.

## Dividing Time When the Two Priorities Genuinely Compete

When project delivery pressure rises and mentoring time is squeezed, three concrete approaches are available, and the right one depends on what is actually under time pressure.

**Shrink the module, don't cancel it.** Where a module is underway and project pressure rises, reduce its scope to the minimum that still meets its stated closure definition (Guide 01's hard rule) rather than deferring it indefinitely. A module that quietly disappears under pressure and is never explicitly rescheduled tends to stay disappeared; a module that is deliberately shrunk stays visible and finishes.

**Reassign, rather than absorb, the Mentor's competing load.** Where a Mentor is being pulled between project responsibilities and mentoring commitments, the correct move is for the Mentor's Manager to temporarily reassign some of the Mentor's other project tasks, per the original Program's guidance, not for the Mentor to quietly work more hours to cover both. A Mentor who absorbs this conflict personally rather than surfacing it is at direct risk of the Over-Extension pattern named in Guide 06.

**Make the trade-off explicit and shared, not unilateral.** Where a genuine choice must be made between a specific mentoring session and a specific project deadline, that choice should be made visibly, by the Mentor and Mentee together, and recorded briefly in the current Module Plan or Curriculum Record (Guide 04) rather than absorbed silently by either party. A Mentee who repeatedly loses mentoring time to project pressure without ever being told this is happening experiences it as neglect; a Mentee who is told directly, and involved in the trade-off, experiences the same lost time as a shared, reasonable constraint.

## When the Conflict Becomes a Pattern, Not an Instance

A single deprioritized session under real, unusual project pressure is normal and does not require escalation. A pattern of mentoring consistently losing to project priorities, across multiple modules or multiple seasons, is a different matter: it indicates the mentoring commitment was never genuinely resourced in the way the original Program's Phase 0 intends, rather than the natural result of one difficult sprint. Where this pattern is confirmed rather than a single instance, it should be raised as a standing conflict rather than repeatedly re-negotiated module by module; see Guide 08 for how to escalate a pattern of this kind.

## Result

A repeatable basis for deciding which tasks belong to mentoring, and three concrete approaches for dividing time when project and mentoring priorities compete in a given moment, together with a threshold for when a recurring version of this conflict stops being a normal scheduling problem and becomes something the framework's escalation path (Guide 08) should address instead.
