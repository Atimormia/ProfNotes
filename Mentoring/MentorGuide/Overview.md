# Mentor Guide: Overview and How to Use This Guide

## Purpose

The Mentoring Program defines the structure: phases, roles, timeline, and documents. This guide is the companion document for Mentors, translating that structure into practical judgment calls. It does not prescribe one correct method. It provides a set of mechanisms for each recurring decision a Mentor faces, along with guidance on when each mechanism fits and how to know when it has worked.

**Specialization-Agnostic by Design:** The Mentoring Program itself is not specific to any one discipline. This guide develops it in full for one discipline, software engineering, as a worked example. Mentors in other disciplines (design, product, management) can use the same toolkits, substituting discipline-specific content where the guide references programming-specific approaches.

## Why a Guide, Not a Script

Fully scripted curricula are rigid. In production environments, a rigid plan does not survive contact with shifting priorities, and the burden of reconciling the plan with reality falls entirely on the Mentee. Fully informal mentoring avoids this rigidity, but frequently leaves the Mentee without a clear sense of direction, and can leave significant, unnoticed gaps in foundational knowledge that no one was tracking.

This guide is built to sit between those two failure modes. Every toolkit offers a menu of mechanisms rather than a single required method, so the Mentor and Mentee can choose an approach that fits their specific working relationship, shared context, and constraints. The one requirement that does not flex: whatever mechanism is chosen, the Mentor and Mentee define what success or closure looks like before starting. Flexibility without a defined evaluation moment is not structure, it is drift with a schedule attached.

## Core Vocabulary: The Iteration Grain

The Mentoring Program operates across four nested levels of iteration. Mentors should be explicit, with themselves and their Mentee, about which grain they are operating at when applying any toolkit in this guide.

* **Stage:** A single unit within a module (a theory discussion, a practice exercise, a review meeting).
* **Module:** A bounded block of the curriculum with its own goals, as defined in the Mentoring Program's Phase 2 and Phase 3.
* **Season:** A full mentoring session, as defined by the Mentoring Program's session periods (e.g., a 3-month or 4-month cycle).
* **Year:** The level at which mentoring outcomes may connect to the company's broader performance review cycle. Year-level results are one input into that process, not a mentoring-specific evaluation of their own.

## The One Hard Rule

Before selecting an approach from any toolkit in this guide, the Mentor and Mentee must agree on how they will know the matter is resolved. This applies equally to diagnosing a gap, choosing a training approach, transferring responsibility, or evaluating progress. A mechanism chosen without a defined closing condition cannot be judged to have worked, regardless of how much effort went into running it.

## Signal Language Used Throughout This Guide

Each toolkit in this guide follows a consistent four-part shape:

1. **Early Signal:** A small, ambiguous cue. On its own, not sufficient grounds to act. Its purpose is to prompt attention, not a response.
2. **Confirmed Pattern:** The point at which a Mentor has enough evidence, through recurrence or clarity, to act with real confidence rather than a guess.
3. **Approach:** The menu of mechanisms available for addressing the confirmed pattern, with guidance on when each fits.
4. **Psychology Note:** Why the pattern tends to occur, and how to relate to the person behind it. Where a pattern is shared across multiple toolkits, this section will point to the Mentee Patterns and Psychology document rather than repeat the explanation.

**A Caution on This Structure:** Mistaking an early signal for a confirmed pattern is a common failure mode in itself. Acting too early risks addressing noise as if it were a real gap; waiting for excessive certainty risks leaving a real gap unaddressed for too long. This guide does not resolve that tension for the Mentor. It is a standing judgment call, and one worth naming explicitly rather than leaving implicit.

## Diagnosis Is Not Performance Review

The Program's iteration grain means diagnostic activity happens often: at minimum, once per module, at whatever grain the module is sized. This frequency exists to give the Mentor and Mentee a running, current picture of direction. It is not a running scorecard, and it should not be treated, by either party or by any stakeholder reviewing the documentation, as a series of graded checkpoints.

Module-level diagnosis (see the Diagnostic Toolkit) answers "where are we, and where should we go next." Season-level synthesis is a distinct act: reading the pattern across a season's module-level diagnoses to understand trajectory, not re-running the diagnostic instrument itself. Year-level results may feed into the company's formal review process as one input among others, but that connection is external to the mentoring relationship and should not distort how module- and season-level diagnosis is conducted or discussed with the Mentee.

## How the Toolkits Are Organized

This guide consists of the following documents. Each addresses a recurring decision point in the mentoring relationship:

1. **Diagnostic Toolkit** — finding and evaluating gaps, at the baseline, module, and season grain.
2. **Training Approach Toolkit** — choosing between planned and reactive curriculum design, and why both are needed.
3. **Responsibility Transfer Toolkit** — deliberately fading support, including AI-specific guidance, as a Mentee's competence grows.
4. **Document Templates and Content Guide** — what belongs in each artifact named by the Mentoring Program, and why each one needs to exist as a written record.
5. **Mentee Patterns and Psychology** — a shared reference of recurring mentee patterns, referenced by the toolkits above rather than repeated within them.
6. **Mentor Growth Toolkit** — what a Mentor should watch for in their own development, including what to do when a Mentor shares the Mentee's gap.
7. **Balancing Teaching and Project Priorities** — deciding which tasks belong to mentoring versus dedicated exercises, and dividing time when the two compete.
8. **When Something Isn't Working** — an evidence-gated escalation path for mentor-mentee friction that does not resolve on its own.

## Result

A shared vocabulary and a small number of standing rules (define success before choosing a method, treat diagnosis as directional rather than evaluative, distinguish an early signal from a confirmed pattern) that every subsequent toolkit in this guide will assume without restating.
