# Mentor Guide 05: Mentoring Psychology Patterns

## Purpose

This is a shared reference of recurring psychological patterns a Mentor is likely to encounter. The toolkits in this series (Guides 01 through 04) point here rather than repeating psychological explanation locally, so a pattern that shows up in gap-finding, responsibility transfer, and evaluation alike only needs to be understood once.

## Why the Specialty Doesn't Matter Here

The patterns in this guide are not specific to software engineering, or to any other discipline this framework supports. Imposter phenomenon, defensive responses to feedback, and unconscious bias in mentoring relationships appear identically across fields as different as medicine, teaching, and software development. What differs by discipline is the content of the work; what does not differ is the psychology underneath it. This is one reason the framework can remain specialization-agnostic at this layer even where it is discipline-specific at the level of exercises and tasks.

## A Note on What This Guide Does Not Provide

This guide deliberately does not offer a personality-to-teaching-style mapping (for example, matching a fixed personality type to a fixed mentoring approach). The available evidence does not strongly support this kind of mapping, and a rigid typology risks becoming a label a Mentor reaches for instead of actually diagnosing what is happening, which runs counter to the method in Guide 01. Where this guide describes a pattern, it describes a recognizable behavioral signature to watch for, not a category to assign to a person.

## Pattern: Mentor Cloning

**Early Signal:** The Mentor finds themselves recommending an approach primarily because it is the one they personally used, rather than because it fits the Mentee's diagnosed gap or working style.

**Confirmed Pattern:** The Mentor is consistently steering the Mentee toward the Mentor's own past choices (the same tools, the same career path, the same problem-solving style) even where the Mentee's diagnosed gap or expressed goals point somewhere else.

**Approach:** Before proposing an approach in any toolkit above, a Mentor should ask explicitly whether they are recommending this because it addresses the diagnosed gap, or because it is familiar to them personally. Where the two overlap, that's a genuine coincidence worth acting on. Where they don't, the diagnosed gap should take precedence.

**Psychology Note:** Mentor cloning does not require the Mentor to think they are doing this. It is a natural default in the absence of active self-checking, since a mentor's own path is the most available example in their own mind. It is worth naming this to oneself explicitly rather than assuming good intentions are sufficient protection against it.

## Pattern: Imposter Pattern

**Early Signal:** A Mentee minimizes a genuine accomplishment in conversation ("it wasn't a big deal," "anyone could have done that"), or appears uncomfortable rather than pleased when acknowledged for something done well.

**Confirmed Pattern:** A recognizable cluster, recurring rather than a one-off: hypersensitivity to critical feedback experienced as evidence of inadequacy rather than information to act on; disproportionate focus on negative feedback over positive; minimizing language about one's own work; and, notably, the pattern intensifying rather than easing after a genuine success, since each new achievement raises the perceived stakes of eventually being "found out."

**Approach:** Do not attempt to argue a Mentee out of this pattern by insisting they are competent; this rarely works and can read as dismissal of a real, felt experience. Instead, anchor feedback in specific, observable evidence rather than general reassurance ("this specific decision showed strong reasoning, here's why" rather than "you're doing great"). Where the pattern intersects with Guide 03's Do Alone stage specifically, be aware that a Mentee experiencing this pattern may under-report their own readiness for the Own It stage even once the evidence supports it; this is exactly why Guide 03's readiness assessment relies on multiple sources rather than the Mentee's self-report alone.

**Psychology Note:** This pattern is documented across a wide range of professions and is not a sign of actual incompetence; a majority of people report experiencing some version of it during their career. It tends to intensify under visibility and after success, not fade with experience on its own, which is worth knowing so a Mentor does not mistake seniority or a strong track record for immunity to it in a given Mentee.

## Pattern: Defensive Response to Feedback

**Early Signal:** A Mentee responds to a small, low-stakes piece of feedback with a reaction disproportionate to its content, deflecting, arguing a minor point at length, or going quiet in a way that reads as withdrawal rather than reflection.

**Confirmed Pattern:** This response recurs across multiple, unrelated pieces of feedback, rather than being specific to one disputed instance where the Mentee may simply have a fair counterpoint.

**Approach:** Read a disproportionate defensive reaction as a signal of perceived threat, not as stubbornness or a character flaw. The Mentee is likely responding to a felt loss of control or a triggered sense of shame, not to the literal content of the feedback. Slow down rather than pressing the original point harder; naming the observation gently ("this seems to have landed harder than I intended, can we come back to it") is often more productive than repeating or defending the original feedback.

**Psychology Note:** This reframe matters practically: treating a defensive reaction as a discipline problem invites more of the same defensiveness, since it confirms the Mentee's sense of being under threat. Treating it as a response to threat invites the Mentee to lower their guard instead.

## Pattern: Affinity Bias in Pairing and Attention

**Early Signal:** A Mentor notices they find it easier, or more natural, to connect with, invest extra time in, or advocate for a Mentee who shares their own background, communication style, or working style, relative to other Mentees.

**Confirmed Pattern:** Across multiple pairings or over time, a Mentor's informal attention, advocacy, or generosity of interpretation consistently favors Mentees who resemble them in some dimension (gender, background, communication style), independent of the Mentees' actual diagnosed gaps or demonstrated skill.

**Approach:** This pattern is not primarily addressed through individual awareness alone; the evidence consistently shows that structure, not good intentions, is what reduces it. This is precisely why this framework relies on a formal matching process, documented and comparable expectations across pairings (Guide 04), and multi-source, criteria-first evaluation (Guide 01) rather than a Mentor's informal, relationship-driven judgment. Where a Mentor notices this pattern in themselves, the most direct mitigation is following the framework's own diagnostic and evaluation steps more rigorously for the affected Mentee, not less, since the structured method is what compensates for what unaided judgment tends to miss.

**Psychology Note:** This is a well-documented, unconscious mechanism, not a description of deliberate prejudice. One study of informal, unassigned mentoring in a technical field found the pairing pattern skewed overwhelmingly toward mentors and mentees who shared the same gender, arising from ordinary personal preference rather than policy. The mechanism is worth naming plainly for exactly that reason: it operates through preferences that feel like nothing more than natural rapport, which is what makes structure, rather than self-monitoring alone, the more reliable safeguard.

## Result

A shared set of four named patterns, each with a recognizable signal and a response grounded in why the pattern occurs, available for the toolkits in Guides 01 through 04 to reference without re-explaining. Where a toolkit above already points here, this guide is the canonical source for that explanation.
